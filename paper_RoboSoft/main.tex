\begin{abstract}
Vision-based tactile sensors need sensor-specific dense supervision, and labeled real contacts are expensive. Real-to-sim-to-real pipelines replace them by rendered contacts, but under a control that is usually missing---the renderer must not see the frames on which the model is tested---we find that a physically calibrated renderer does not improve dense prediction, that a learned renderer needs about $50$ labeled pairs per object before it does, and that the common leaky protocol overstates its benefit by $2$--$4\times$. Simulation does provide exact geometry; what is missing is the pose of that geometry in each real frame. We therefore propose \emph{SnapTac}, mesh-aligned pseudo-labeling, which turns unlabeled real frames, free to capture, into labeled ones: the $\Kreal$-shot model proposes a pose for each frame, a residual aligner trained on the same $\Kreal$ labeled frames snaps the object mesh onto the frame, and the mesh depth at the aligned pose becomes its label. With only $10$--$25$ labeled frames per object (less than a tenth of the training frames), SnapTac lowers contact error by $13$--$20\%$ on a \sensor{} sensor and by $33$--$36\%$ on a \sensorB{} sensor over real-only training. The gain persists up to $100$ labels, where SnapTac surpasses the leaky renderer; it halves the labels needed for a given error on both sensors and carries over to a second capture session. Applied at test time, the same aligner refines the predicted pose from $3$--$10^\circ$ to about $1^\circ$. The obvious alternatives---self-training on the model's own predictions and a render-and-compare search with the few-shot renderer---do not improve contact localization.
\end{abstract}
