\begin{abstract}
Vision-based tactile sensors need sensor-specific dense supervision, and labeled real contacts are expensive. Real-to-sim-to-real pipelines replace them by rendered contacts, but under a control that is usually missing---the renderer must not see the frames on which the downstream model is tested---we find that a physically calibrated renderer does not improve dense prediction, that a learned renderer needs about $50$ labeled pairs per object before it does, and that the common leaky protocol overstates its benefit by $2$--$4\times$. What simulation does provide is exact geometry. We therefore propose \emph{mesh-aligned pseudo-labeling} (MAPL), which turns unlabeled real frames, free to capture, into labeled ones: the $\Kreal$-shot model proposes a pose for each frame, a residual aligner trained on the same $\Kreal$ labeled frames snaps the object mesh onto the frame, and the mesh depth at the aligned pose becomes its label. On a \sensor{} fingertip sensor with 12 objects and $\Kreal=10$--$100$ labeled frames per object, MAPL lowers depth MSE by $13$--$37\%$ and contact error by $13$--$28\%$ over real-only training, surpasses the leaky renderer at $\Kreal=100$, halves the labels needed for a given depth error, and carries over to a second capture session ($-28\%$ contact error) and to a second sensor (\sensorB{}, $-39\%$ contact error with $25$ labels). Applied at test time, the same aligner refines the predicted pose from $3$--$10^\circ$ to about $1^\circ$. Self-training on the model's own predictions, a render-and-compare search with the few-shot renderer and an unpaired sim-to-real refiner all fall short of it.
\end{abstract}
