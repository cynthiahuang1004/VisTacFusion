\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

\noindent\textbf{What simulation buys, and for which task.}
\emph{Pose} needs coverage of the object's contact silhouettes over the pose space, which the geometry stage provides regardless of appearance: a physical renderer fitted to one no-contact frame makes the pose of a new object estimable, covering the rotation range matters more than rendering quality, and simulation improves both pose and contact localization across sessions. \emph{Depth and normals} depend on the appearance inside the contact, which only a learned renderer reproduces, and only after $\approx\!25$--$50$ labeled contacts per object; below that nothing leakage-free helps, above $\approx\!100$ real data alone is already good. A domain-randomized physical renderer that uses no real data at all matches the calibrated one for depth but not pose, and is a practical zero-cost baseline when calibration is impractical. \emph{Classification} of unseen objects does not transfer through simulation, as the identity features in the contact appearance lie entirely in the appearance regime that no leakage-free renderer reproduces. Choose by task: for pose of known meshes, calibrate a physical renderer from a background frame and cover the rotations; for dense geometry, budget the real pairs the learned renderer needs, condition it on the session background so that new sessions and objects cost one frame, and account for those pairs when reporting.

\noindent\textbf{Practical recipe.}
Given a new sensor with CAD models of the target objects:
\emph{(1)}~Capture one no-contact frame per session and calibrate the physical renderer (or use domain randomization if calibration is impractical).
\emph{(2)}~For pose, render every object at every reachable rotation; this alone takes the unseen-object pose error from chance to $5$--$7^\circ$, and further improvement comes from covering more rotations, not from higher rendering quality.
\emph{(3)}~For dense depth and normals, budget $\approx\!50$ labeled real contacts per object and train a background-conditioned learned renderer on those same pairs; below $25$ the renderer adds nothing leakage-free. Capture unlabeled frames freely: pseudo-label them with the $\Kreal$-shot model, verify the labels against the meshes by render-and-compare, and give them to the model (and the renderer); this buys $14$--$25\%$ depth MSE and $7$--$14\%$ contact MAE, and without meshes still $7$--$21\%$ depth MSE.
\emph{(4)}~When adding the sensor to a new session, capture one background frame and condition the renderer on it---this is the cheapest extension that helps unseen-object depth ($-13\%$).
\emph{(5)}~Report the renderer's real budget and keep it disjoint from the downstream test frames; on our data the common leaky protocol overstates the depth benefit by $2$--$4\times$.
\emph{(6)}~Screen renderer variants with the contact-region fidelity proxy before committing to downstream training.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting.}
Learned-renderer results should state the renderer's real training set and keep it disjoint from the downstream test frames. On our data the leaky protocol overstates the depth benefit by $2$--$4\times$ where the pipeline is most attractive, and manufactures a $30\%$ gain on unseen objects that no leakage-free renderer reproduces. The contact-region fidelity proxy makes the strict protocol cheap: every renderer variant was scored in seconds before any downstream training, and the ranking held for the 14 strict paired renderers that were then trained ($\rho=0.95$).

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.}
Sensor-A data have one session per object, so object identity and session appearance are confounded in the leave-object-out study; the second session and sensor separate them only partially (re-estimated poses, a different capture protocol). Depth references are mesh projections, not membrane measurements. The downstream model is one frozen-encoder architecture (SITR-B/18, pre-trained on tactile data); replacing it with Depth Anything v2 or T3 Large gives comparable depth ($\pm5\%$ contact MAE) but substantially worse pose on unseen objects ($14.3^\circ$ and $16.4^\circ$ vs.\ $6.8^\circ$), suggesting that SITR's tactile pretraining helps the geometry features that drive pose transfer. A trainable encoder may extract more from synthetic data or be more sensitive to its artifacts. The verification step of PPB needs the object mesh and identity of every unlabeled frame (free here, since simulation uses the same meshes), keeps a fixed half of the mesh labels, and its fit is limited by the few-shot renderer (label IoU $0.64$ at $\Kreal=25$ against $0.95$ with the true pose); it was not run on sensor B. The physical renderer uses a geometric gel model; a mechanical model could improve the contact shading we identify as the bottleneck.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.}
We compared a physically calibrated and a learned tactile renderer on two fingertip sensors, four tasks and four transfer protocols, under a rule that charges each renderer for the real data it consumes and forbids it from seeing the test frames. Simulation transfers tactile pose estimation to objects without real data and stabilizes it across sessions with a renderer calibrated from a single frame; dense depth and normals improve only with a learned renderer and a moderate real budget, on both sensors, and the benefit is overstated by up to $4\times$ when the renderer sees the test distribution. Object classification ($94\%$ on seen objects) never transfers through simulation---the sim-to-real gap in contact appearance is too large for identity features. A background-conditioned learned renderer and rotation coverage extend the useful range at no extra labeled data, domain randomization matches calibration for depth without any real data, while physics-guided, illumination-normalized and difference-image renderers do not help. Pseudo-pair bootstrapping turns unlabeled real frames into the leaky renderer's depth gain at the same label budget, while a zero-label unpaired refiner does not. Self-training alone leaves contact localization where the labeled budget put it; verifying each pseudo label by render-and-compare against the mesh---which the simulation already owns---is what lowers contact and pose error.
