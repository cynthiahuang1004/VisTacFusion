\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

\noindent\textbf{What simulation buys, and for which task.}
The two renderer families answer different needs. The physically calibrated renderer, fitted to one no-contact frame, is enough to make the pose of a \emph{new object} estimable from touch ($48^\circ\!\to\!5.6^\circ$) as soon as its mesh is available, because pose needs coverage of the object's contact silhouettes in the pose space, which the geometry stage provides regardless of appearance. The learned renderer is the only one that lowers dense contact-depth error, and it does so only after $\approx100$ labeled contacts per object---a budget at which real-only training is already reasonable. Practitioners choosing a renderer should therefore ask which task they need: for pose or contact-region localization of known meshes, calibrate a physical renderer from a background frame; for dense geometry, budget the real pairs the learned renderer needs, and account for them.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting.}
We argue that learned-renderer results should state the renderer's real training set and keep it disjoint from the downstream test frames. On our data the leaky protocol overstates the depth benefit by $2\times$ in the few-shot regime and manufactures a $19\%$ gain on unseen objects that no leakage-free renderer reproduces.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.}
All real data come from one sensor and one session per object, so object identity and session appearance are confounded; a multi-session capture would separate cross-object from cross-session generalization, and we found the session background to vary more than the renderer error. The depth references are mesh projections at the recorded pose, not membrane measurements, so ``contact MAE'' measures agreement with the commanded contact rather than the deformed gel. The downstream model is a single frozen-encoder architecture; a trainable encoder may extract more from synthetic data, or be more sensitive to its artifacts. Results are single-seed; 150-epoch reruns of four key configurations changed no ranking, and multi-seed tables are in progress. Finally, our physical renderer uses a geometric gel model; a mechanical model could improve the contact shading that we identify as the bottleneck.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.}
We compared a physically calibrated and a learned tactile renderer on one fingertip sensor under a protocol that charges each renderer for the real data it consumes and forbids it from seeing the test frames. Simulation is decisive for transferring tactile pose estimation to objects without real data, with a renderer calibrated from a single frame; for dense contact depth, the benefit is gated by contact-region appearance fidelity, appears only with $\Kreal\gtrsim100$ real pairs per object, and is overstated by up to $2\times$ when the renderer sees the test distribution. A contact-region fidelity proxy predicts downstream ranking and allows renderer design to be iterated without downstream training; using it, we showed that physics pretraining and illumination normalization do not close the few-shot gap, which lies in the shading inside the contact.
