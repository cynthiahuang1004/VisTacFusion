\section{Introduction}

Contact geometry tells a robot where an object meets its fingertip and how the object is oriented. Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight, GelSlim and DIGIT~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim,lambeta2020digit} infer this geometry from images of a deforming elastomer. Their appearance varies with illumination, elastomer properties and camera response, so a predictor needs accurately labeled examples from the target sensor. We study known-object perception: object identity and a mesh are available, but registering every real contact to that mesh is costly.

Simulation reduces manual registration by generating tactile images and geometric labels from known meshes~\cite{wang2022tacto,si2022taxim,chen2023tacchi}. Learned renderers can narrow the gap to the target sensor, but fitting them also consumes real image--label pairs. If a renderer sees more real contacts than the downstream predictor, its synthetic data carries information outside the stated predictor budget. We therefore count renderer supervision as part of the real training-label budget.

Unlabeled tactile images already contain the target sensor's appearance. With a known mesh, labeling one of these images reduces to estimating its contact state: in-plane rotation, translation and indentation. A model trained on a small registered set can initialize this state, but even modest errors shift the projected contact and corrupt its dense targets. The mesh must therefore be aligned before it can supervise the image.

MeshTac learns this alignment as a residual correction (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapl}). Given a real image and the mesh depth at a candidate state, the aligner updates the state iteratively. Perturbing registered contacts supplies its training examples without further labeling. The corrected mesh then provides depth, normal and pose targets for the real image, allowing the student to learn from real appearance and predict without mesh rendering at standard inference.

We compare real-only training, synthetic augmentation, self-training and MeshTac under matched real-label budgets. The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A mesh-aligned pseudo-labeling method that learns contact-state corrections from a small registered set and converts unlabeled real images into depth, normal and pose supervision.
    \item A label-accounting protocol that assigns the renderer and predictor the same real training-label budget, separating synthetic-data gains from access to additional paired contacts.
    \item Experiments on two sensors, a second capture session and held-out objects that identify the complementary roles of alignment and simulation, and evaluate the aligner for pose refinement without retraining.
\end{itemize}
