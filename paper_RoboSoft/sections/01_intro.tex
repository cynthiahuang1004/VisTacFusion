\section{Introduction}

Tactile perception provides robots with contact feedback for dexterous manipulation and physical interaction. Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight, GelSlim and DIGIT~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim,lambeta2020digit} use a soft elastomer membrane to convert fine contact-induced deformations into high-resolution visual signals. Learning to recover contact geometry and pose supports perception through touch, but requires scalable, accurately labeled data. Calibration and registration must link each tactile image to physical quantities such as dense contact-depth maps. A known object mesh can supply these labels only after its contact state is registered to the image.

In real data collection (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}a), repeatable pressing motions do not guarantee submillimeter image--geometry registration. Systematic kinematic errors can bias the estimated contact state, including on Franka manipulators~\cite{franzese2025mukca}, while sensor mounting and object placement introduce additional uncertainty. Registering individual contacts is labor-intensive, and changes in sensor configuration or capture conditions may require re-registration, limiting the labeled set.

Simulation and self-training offer two ways to use a small labeled set. Simulation generates labeled contacts from known geometry~\cite{wang2022tacto,si2022taxim,chen2023tacchi} (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}b), but reproducing marker-free appearance across the tactile surface is challenging: illumination, surface reflectance and contact-induced deformation jointly determine the image~\cite{si2022taxim,agarwal2021simulation}. A renderer fitted on real image--geometry pairs can adapt to the sensor, but those pairs consume the label budget. Self-training instead uses a predictor's outputs to label remaining real images and can propagate its errors~\cite{arazo2020pseudo}.

We instead use real tactile images for appearance and simulated contact geometry for supervision. For a known object, its mesh specifies the geometry, but dense labels depend on a low-dimensional contact state: in-plane rotation, translation and indentation. Accurate state estimation makes the mesh projections useful as labels for the real image. The key is therefore to learn geometric alignment rather than reproduce tactile appearance; simply placing the mesh at an imperfect teacher prediction is insufficient.

AlignTac learns this alignment from a small registered set (Figs.~\ref{fig:teaser}c and~\ref{fig:mapl}). We perturb each contact's mesh state while keeping its real image fixed, and train a residual aligner to recover the registered state. For additional real contacts, a teacher provides the initial state, the aligner corrects it using the observed image, and the aligned geometry supplies depth, normal and planar-pose pseudo-labels. A student learns from these labels and predicts without mesh rendering.

We evaluate AlignTac against self-training and synthetic augmentation under limited real-label budgets on two tactile sensors. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A real-image--geometry alignment framework that learns state corrections from perturbations of a small registered set and generates dense geometry and pose supervision for unlabeled real images without synthetic tactile RGB.
    \item Evidence that accurate contact-state alignment, not access to geometry alone, improves pseudo-labels and student accuracy, with label efficiency on two sensors and robustness on a second session.
    \item A matched-budget renderer study identifying complementary benefits of tactile RGB simulation for held-out-object pose coverage and session changes, while accounting for the real labels used to fit the renderer.
\end{itemize}
