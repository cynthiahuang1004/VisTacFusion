\section{Introduction}

Contact geometry and pose describe where an object touches a fingertip and its in-plane orientation. Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight, GelSlim and DIGIT~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim,lambeta2020digit} record these contacts as images of a deforming elastomer. Training a model to recover geometry from the images requires paired labels. A known object mesh can supply these labels, but each real contact must first be registered to the mesh.

Accurate contact geometry is particularly important for soft fingertips: a compliant pad touches an object over an area, so grasp and in-hand manipulation decisions depend on where the pad touches and at what orientation. Registering individual contacts is labor-intensive, and changes in sensor configuration or capture conditions may require re-registration, limiting the number of labeled contacts.

Simulation and self-training offer two ways to use a small labeled set. Simulation generates labeled contacts from known geometry~\cite{wang2022tacto,si2022taxim,chen2023tacchi}. A renderer fitted on real image--geometry pairs can adapt their appearance to the target sensor, but those pairs consume part of the label budget. Self-training instead uses a predictor's outputs to label the remaining real images and can propagate its errors~\cite{arazo2020pseudo}.

For known objects, unlabeled real images provide a complementary source of supervision. Their appearance already matches the sensor; the unknown is the contact state---in-plane rotation, translation and indentation. A teacher trained on the registered set can estimate this state. However, even an exact mesh produces incorrect dense labels if its placement is wrong. Improving the state estimate is therefore necessary before using the mesh as supervision.

MeshTac learns this correction from the registered contacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapl}). We perturb their states and train a residual aligner to recover them from the real image and candidate mesh depth. Applied to the teacher's estimates, the aligner places the mesh before generating depth, normal and pose pseudo-labels. A student learns from these labels and predicts without mesh rendering.

We evaluate mesh-aligned supervision against self-training and synthetic augmentation under limited real-label budgets on two tactile sensors. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A geometry-constrained pseudo-labeling strategy that turns a small set of registered contacts into dense depth, normal and planar-pose supervision for additional real tactile images, without synthetic tactile RGB generation.
    \item Evidence that accurate contact-state alignment, not access to the mesh alone, is critical for pseudo-label quality and student accuracy, with label efficiency shown on two sensors and robustness on a second session.
    \item A matched-budget renderer study showing where simulation is complementary: geometric coverage for held-out objects and session changes, with gains that must be weighed against the real labels used to fit the renderer.
\end{itemize}
