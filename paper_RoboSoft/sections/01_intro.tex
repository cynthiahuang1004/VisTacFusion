\section{Introduction}

Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight, GelSlim and DIGIT~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim,lambeta2020digit} measure contact by imaging a soft elastomer under internal colored illumination. The image is not a picture of the object: it is the product of gel deformation, LED placement and color, elastomer reflectance, camera response and manufacturing variation. Two sensors of the same design, or the same sensor across sessions, produce different images for the same contact. Dense perception from these images---contact depth, surface normals, object pose---therefore needs sensor-specific supervision, and dense real labels are expensive.

Real-to-sim-to-real pipelines address this by \emph{calibrating a tactile renderer} on a small amount of real data and training on synthetic images whose depth, normal and pose labels are free~\cite{si2022taxim,wang2022tacto,chen2023tacchi,zhong2025tactgen}. Two renderer families are in use. \emph{Physically calibrated} renderers keep a light-transport model of the sensor and fit its parameters to real images~\cite{agarwal2021simulation,si2022taxim}; they can be calibrated from a single no-contact frame. \emph{Learned} renderers replace the light-transport model by an image-to-image network trained on real (depth, image) pairs~\cite{isola2017pix2pix,church2022tactile,zhong2025tactgen}; they need labeled contacts, i.e.\ the data the pipeline is meant to save.

This paper asks how much real data each renderer needs before its synthetic data improves a downstream tactile model, for each of the three tasks, and whether the reported gains survive a control that is usually missing: \emph{the renderer must not see the real frames on which the downstream model is tested}. In many learned-renderer pipelines the renderer is trained on all available real pairs and the downstream model is validated on frames from the same sessions; the renderer has then memorized the appearance of the test contacts and the synthetic data carries it into training. We show this leakage inflates the measured depth benefit by $2$--$4\times$.

We study the question on \sensor{}, a GelSlim-type fingertip sensor, with one downstream architecture (a frozen sensor-invariant encoder~\cite{gupta2025sitr} with dense depth/normal and planar-pose heads) and two renderers built on the same meshes and contact sampler: a Blender renderer calibrated by Bayesian optimization to one no-contact image, and a pix2pix depth-to-image renderer trained on $\Kreal$ real pairs per object. We evaluate under four protocols. \emph{Leave-object-out} holds out three objects with no real data. \emph{$\Kreal$-shot} gives every object $\Kreal\in\{0,10,25,50,100\}$ real frames and trains the learned renderer on the same frames as the downstream model. \emph{Cross-session} tests every model on a second capture of the same objects, and \emph{cross-sensor} repeats the $\Kreal$-shot study on a \sensorB{} with its own real pairs. The curves then motivate our method, \emph{pseudo-pair bootstrapping} (PPB): the $\Kreal$-shot model labels \emph{unlabeled} real frames, which cost nothing to capture, each label is verified against the object mesh by render-and-compare with the $\Kreal$-shot renderer, and the verified pairs retrain both the learned renderer and the model; we compare it with a zero-label alternative, a contact-preserving unpaired refiner of the physical render, under the same rule.

Our findings (150 training epochs; three seeds where gaps are small) are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pose transfers to new objects through geometry; depth and normals do not; classification never transfers.} Simulated contacts of a new mesh take rotation error from chance ($65^\circ$) to $6$--$7^\circ$ with a renderer that never saw a contact, and a control without the meshes stays at $51^\circ$; contact depth on the same objects moves by at most $4\%$ unless the renderer has seen them. Object classification ($94\%$ on seen objects) scores $0\%$ on the three held-out objects regardless of the renderer.
    \item \textbf{Dense geometry needs a learned renderer and $\Kreal\gtrsim50$.} Depth MSE and contact MAE fall by $10$--$18\%$ at $\Kreal\ge50$ with a strict learned renderer, by nothing at $\Kreal\le25$, and never with the physical renderer, which costs $0.5$--$1.5^\circ$ of rotation instead. The same curve reappears on a second sensor with its own pairs ($-29\%$ contact MAE at $\Kreal=25$).
    \item \textbf{Cheap extensions and a zero-cost baseline.} Conditioning the learned renderer on the session background image improves unseen-object depth by $13\%$ for one background frame, covering each object's rotation range in simulation lowers unseen-object pose error from $10.3^\circ$ to $4.4$--$6.8^\circ$, and a domain-randomized physical renderer that needs no real data matches the calibrated one for depth.
    \item \textbf{Across sessions, simulation helps every task.} On a second capture of the same objects, adding simulation raises contact IoU and lowers rotation error by a third for the full-data model, and the gain again traces to mesh coverage.
    \item \textbf{Leakage and a fidelity proxy.} The leaky renderer reports $24$--$31\%$ depth gains at every $\Kreal$; the strict numbers are $0$--$16\%$. A contact-region image error ranks the renderer variants like their downstream error (Spearman $\rho=0.95$), and shows that four physics-guided renderers, illumination normalization and difference imaging do not close the few-shot gap.
    \item \textbf{Pseudo-pair bootstrapping turns unlabeled frames into the leaky gain.} PPB lowers depth MSE by $14$--$25\%$ and contact MAE by $7$--$14\%$ over real-only training at the same label budget, matches the leaky reference at $\Kreal=100$ (within $1\%$), and gives the best leakage-free pose at $\Kreal=25$. Its ablation shows that the frames are worth more to the model than to the renderer, that a second round and a zero-label unpaired refiner do not help, and that the render-and-compare verification is what moves contact error: without it, pseudo labels inherit the labeler's localization error.
\end{enumerate}
