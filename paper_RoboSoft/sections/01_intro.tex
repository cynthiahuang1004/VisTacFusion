\section{Introduction}

Contact geometry tells a robot where an object meets its fingertip and how it is oriented. Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight, GelSlim and DIGIT~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim,lambeta2020digit} capture it through images of a deforming elastomer, and supervised geometry prediction needs labels paired with these sensor-specific images. For known objects, meshes can provide the labels, but each real contact must first be registered to its mesh.

Two common remedies address limited labeling budgets. Simulation generates labeled contacts from known geometry~\cite{wang2022tacto,si2022taxim,chen2023tacchi}, but learned renderers reduce its appearance gap only with real image--label pairs of the target sensor, which belong in the training-label budget. Self-training uses a model trained on the few registered contacts to pseudo-label the remaining real images, but inherits that model's errors~\cite{arazo2020pseudo}.

Our key observation is that unlabeled real images already capture the target sensor's appearance; with a known mesh, the missing information is the contact state---in-plane rotation, translation and indentation. A teacher trained on the registered set can estimate this state, but projecting the mesh at an inaccurate state shifts the contact region and corrupts the dense labels; the state must be corrected before the mesh can supervise.

MeshTac corrects the contact state before generating labels (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapl}). A residual aligner iteratively updates the state from the real image and candidate mesh depth. Perturbing registered contacts supplies training examples without additional real labels. The aligned mesh provides depth, normal and pose targets for a student that predicts without mesh rendering.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A mesh-aligned pseudo-labeling method that learns contact-state corrections from a small registered set and generates geometric supervision for unlabeled real images.
    \item A controlled renderer study showing that the apparent benefit of synthetic tactile augmentation depends strongly on whether the real image--label pairs used to train the renderer are counted in the supervision budget.
    \item Experiments on two sensors and a second capture session that evaluate MeshTac's label efficiency and robustness, a separate held-out-object study of when synthetic supervision helps, and an evaluation of the aligner for test-time pose refinement.
\end{itemize}
