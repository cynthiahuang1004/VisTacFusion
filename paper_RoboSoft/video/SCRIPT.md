# AlignTac video script  (planned 178 s ≈ 2:58; narration ~475 words)

| # | s | slide | narration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | (title slide) | AlignTac: aligning real tactile images with simulated geometry for label-efficient contact learning. |
| 2 | 14 | Tactile images are easy to collect; their labels are not | Tactile sensors turn contact into detailed images, but learning geometry from them needs dense labels. Each label is the object mesh projected at a calibrated, verified robot pose, redone whenever the object moves. Images are cheap; labels are not. |
| 3 | 12 | Simulation and self-training each pay a different price | Simulation gives geometry for free, but its renderer must be fitted to the sensor with labeled real pairs. Self-training reuses the predictor's own outputs and inherits its errors. |
| 4 | 14 | AlignTac: keep the real appearance, align the known geometry | Our insight: the real image already holds the appearance, and the known mesh already holds the geometry. Only a four-dimensional contact state is missing. AlignTac learns to align the mesh with each real image. |
| 5 | 16 | Step 1: a teacher and an aligner from K labeled contacts | From K labeled contacts per object, twenty-five here, we train a teacher for depth, normals and pose, and an aligner: perturb the known contact state, project the mesh there, and learn to recover the perturbation from the image and the candidate depth. |
| 6 | 16 | Steps 2 and 3: pseudo-label the unlabeled images, train the student | For each unlabeled image, the teacher gives an initial state and the aligner refines it in six updates. The mesh at the final state is the pseudo-label. A student trains on both sets and predicts from the image and object identity alone. |
| 7 | 16 | Alignment on held-out real contacts | On held-out frames, red is the mesh at the teacher's state, several degrees off the ridges. Each update moves it; after six, the green outline matches the ground-truth region in white. Over all held-out frames, the mean rotation error falls from 5.7 to 1.2 degrees in three updates. |
| 8 | 14 | Alignment turns known geometry into accurate labels | Alignment is what makes geometry useful. With the same teacher, the mesh at the teacher's pose gives labels with IoU 0.47, worse than teacher depth. Aligned, the IoU is 0.72 and rotation error falls from six degrees to one. |
| 9 | 14 | Label efficiency on two GelSlim 4.0 variants | The student trained on aligned geometry has the lowest contact error at every budget on both variants. With 25 labels per object, error drops twenty and thirty-six percent on the two sensors, matching real-only training with twice the labels. |
| 10 | 14 | Contact depth and normals on held-out frames | The real-only model blurs or breaks thin ridges and misplaces junctions; the AlignTac student recovers them, also on everyday shapes: a button, a hex key, an edge and a marble. Where depth errors are similar, the surface normals still show the difference. |
| 11 | 16 | Robust across sessions, sensor variants and objects | On a second session with re-placed objects, error stays fourteen to twenty-one percent lower. Pseudo-labeling the new images with the same aligner, with no poses, lowers it a further five percent; on four new objects the gain is thirteen percent. |
| 12 | 12 | The aligner also refines pose at test time | The same aligner also refines pose at inference: fifteen-degree perturbations, twice the training range, shrink to half a degree, and refining each predictor's own pose lowers its error on both sessions. |
| 13 | 12 | Takeaways | In short: real images provide appearance, aligned geometry provides supervision. A few labeled contacts learn the alignment, which labels many more images, transfers to a new session, and refines pose at test time. Code will be released. Thank you. |
