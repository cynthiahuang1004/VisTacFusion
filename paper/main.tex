\begin{abstract}
Vision and touch provide complementary cues for object pose and local contact geometry, but external cameras are often occluded at the contact site. We present \sensor{}, a fingertip sensor that acquires visual and tactile observations with one fixed camera and a continuous UV-responsive contact layer. By switching illumination, the camera sequentially acquires through-membrane visual images and contact-sensitive tactile images from a shared viewpoint, avoiding inter-camera extrinsic calibration. To combine these observations, we introduce \method{}, a multimodal transformer with separate prediction paths for planar object pose and contact geometry. Asymmetric bottleneck fusion incorporates visual context while preserving tactile spatial features for depth and surface-normal estimation. Modality dropout enables inference with paired inputs or either modality alone. On real validation data from 12 known objects, paired inputs reduce depth MSE, angular error, and translation $L_1$ by 61.2\%, 72.3\%, and 61.3\%, respectively, relative to tactile-only inference with the same model. Relative to visual-only inference, the corresponding reductions are 93.7\%, 6.1\%, and 8.6\%. \href{https://gelslim5.github.io/geslim5_website/}
{\textcolor{blue}{Project website}}
\end{abstract}

Contact-rich manipulation requires estimating both object pose and the local geometry of contact. Vision supplies object context, while optical tactile sensors such as GelSight and GelSlim capture detailed contact structure~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,donlon2018gelslim}. The modalities can resolve different ambiguities: visual context can distinguish object poses that produce similar contact patches, while tactile measurements reveal local surface geometry that visual appearance alone may not resolve.\unskip

With external cameras, however, the hand and object can occlude the contact site, and viewpoint differences complicate spatial association with tactile measurements. A shared fingertip camera places visual sensing at the contact interface and provides a common frame for relating object appearance to contact geometry. Prior visuotactile sensors obtain scene access through semitransparent skins~\cite{hogan2021sts}, controllable films~\cite{luu2023protac}, UV-visible markers~\cite{wang2022spectac}, or spatial multiplexing~\cite{hu2026muxgel}.
Building on these designs, we couple a continuous contact interface with an estimator for object pose and spatially resolved contact geometry.\unskip

We introduce \sensor{}, a fingertip sensor that co-locates vision and touch within the GelSlim~4.0 body design~\cite{sipos2024gelslim4}. A continuous UV-responsive contact layer allows one fixed camera to record through-membrane visual images with UV off and contact-sensitive tactile images with UV on (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).
The observations are acquired sequentially under approximately stationary contact and share a viewpoint and image coordinates, supporting spatial association without inter-camera extrinsic calibration.

To exploit these complementary observations, \method{} uses separate prediction paths for planar object pose and contact geometry. Tactile-initialized queries provide a pose estimate, while intermediate tactile features preserve spatial detail for contact-depth and surface-normal estimation. A shared visual bottleneck conditions both paths, allowing object context to inform pose and geometry predictions. Modality dropout trains the model to operate with either input alone or the pair.

Our contributions are \textbf{(1)} a fingertip sensor that co-locates vision and touch using a continuous UV-responsive contact interface; \textbf{(2)} an estimator that combines visual context with separate pose queries and spatial geometry features; and \textbf{(3)} an evaluation on 12 known objects covering modality complementarity, encoder and fusion choices, and training configuration. Within-session validation shows lower contact-depth and pose errors for paired observations than for either input alone in the same estimator.\unskip

\noindent\textbf{From tactile fingers to switchable visuotactile sensing.} GelSight, DIGIT, and the GelSlim family image a deformable contact interface~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,lambeta2020digit,donlon2018gelslim,ma2019gelslim2,taylor2022gelslim3,sipos2024gelslim4}. GelSlim~4.0 emphasizes reproducible hardware and perception~\cite{sipos2024gelslim4}; \sensor{} retains its body design and changes the contact film and illumination to add a visual observation state. Scene access has also been obtained with semitransparent interfaces in STS, VisTac, and StereoTac~\cite{hogan2021sts,athar2023vistac,roberge2023stereotac}; UV-visible markers in SpecTac and TransTac~\cite{wang2022spectac,yang2026transtac}; PDLC control in ProTac~\cite{luu2023protac}; and mechanical switching in Look-to-Touch~\cite{dong2025looktotouch}. ViTacTip and M$^3$Tac use transparent or spectrally selective constructions~\cite{fan2024vitactip,li2024m3tac}. MuxGel spatially interleaves transparent and tactile regions for single-camera reconstruction~\cite{hu2026muxgel}, while FingerEye uses binocular visuotactile sensing for manipulation~\cite{xu2026fingereye}. Our implementation combines a continuous UV-responsive outer contact film, sequential same-camera acquisition, and learned pose/contact estimation within the GelSlim body. The estimator uses contact-sensitive image appearance rather than marker displacement, without spatially partitioning the film into clear and coated windows. Table~\ref{tab:sensor_design_comparison} compares construction, acquisition, and contact representation; head dimensions and contact-interface measurements are listed \mbox{separately within the size column.}\unskip

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Learning from visual and tactile representations}
Visual pretraining methods such as MAE, DINO, Depth Anything, CLIP, and SigLIP provide distinct feature representations~\cite{he2022mae,oquab2023dinov2,simeoni2025dinov3,yang2024dav2,radford2021clip,zhai2023siglip}. Tactile and cross-modal representations include Sparsh, T3, SITR, TVL, and Sparsh-X~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,zhao2024transferable,gupta2025sitr,fu2024tvl,higuera2025sparshx}, while MViTac learns visual--tactile alignment by contrastive pretraining~\cite{dave2024mvitac}. We use these representations for object-pose and contact-geometry estimation, rather than introducing a new backbone-pretraining objective. Building on attention bottlenecks~\cite{nagrani2021mbt} and DPT-style dense decoding~\cite{ranftl2021dpt}, \method{} uses compact visual context to condition a pose path and a separate contact-geometry decoder. Simulators such as TACTO motivate supplementing real tactile data with synthetic observations~\cite{wang2022tacto}; our evaluation uses Blender-based co-training, not simulation-only transfer.\unskip

\sensor{} is a monocular visual--tactile fingertip sensor that captures both observation modalities from a single camera by switching UV illumination (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor}). The design has three elements: (i)~a compliant contact membrane whose optical state changes under UV, (ii)~an illumination ring that drives the switching, and (iii)~a 3D-printed body housing one camera and a custom driver PCB. Below, we describe each element and the resulting acquisition protocol.

\noindent\textbf{Compliant contact interface.} The prototype retains the GelSlim~4.0 gripper-body CAD~\cite{sipos2024gelslim4} and uses a two-layer Solaris (Smooth-On) silicone membrane. We form a 5\,mm clear base by mixing the silicone 1:1 by weight, vacuum-degassing, and casting it in a mold. The continuous UV-responsive layer is spin-coated from 3\,g Solaris mixed with 0.3\,mL invisible UV-reactive ink (United Nuclear). This layer contacts the object directly, without a separate protective overlayer.\unskip

The 3D-printed PLA body has a nominal circular sensing area of 30\,mm diameter and houses a Raspberry Pi camera. An illumination ring uses UV LEDs in place of the blue channel; red and green illumination remain available. A custom driver PCB and a GPIO-controlled MOSFET switch the UV channel and coordinate image capture. \unskip

\section{LEARNING OBJECT POSE AND CONTACT GEOMETRY} \label{sec:method}

\method{} combines visual context with tactile signatures through separate pose and geometry prediction paths (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}).
The pose path aggregates features into a pose vector, while the geometry path preserves spatial detail for depth and normals. Cross-attention associates the modalities without requiring pixelwise correspondence.

\subsection{Problem Formulation} \label{sec:problem}

For a known object $o$, let $I_v$ and $I_t$ denote the image pair acquired under the quasi-static protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:sensor}. The input $\mathcal I_m$ is $I_v$, $I_t$, or $(I_v,I_t)$ for modes $m\in\{\mathrm{Vis},\mathrm{Tac},\mathrm{Both}\}$, respectively. We learn an estimator with parameters $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
 f_\phi(\mathcal I_m,o)=\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf y},\widehat D,\widehat{\mathbf N}\bigr),
 \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf y=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,t_x,t_y)$ encodes sensor-relative planar pose. Depth $D\in\mathbb R^{H\times W}$ and surface normals $\mathbf N\in\mathbb R^{H\times W\times3}$ describe contact geometry on the tactile image grid. Translations are normalized as in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}; object identity $o$ is supplied to the pose queries.

\subsection{Modality-Specific Features}
\label{sec:encoders}
Frozen SITR-B/16~\cite{gupta2025sitr} and MAE-L/16~\cite{he2022mae} encode touch and vision, respectively. At $224\times224$ resolution, each produces 196 patch tokens and one CLS token, with widths 768 and 1024. SITR uses a fixed set of 18 calibration references; calibration tokens are discarded before downstream prediction.

Separate linear projections map both streams to width 768 and add modality embeddings. Tactile patch tokens receive spatial embeddings and initialize 196 spatial queries; the projected CLS token initializes one pose query. All queries receive an object-identity embedding. Projected visual tokens form fixed memory $M\in\mathbb R^{197\times768}$. All downstream modules are trained jointly.

\subsection{Object Pose Estimation}
\label{sec:trunk}
Tactile-initialized queries $Q_0\in\mathbb R^{197\times768}$ read visual context through 16 learned bottleneck tokens $B_0\in\mathbb R^{16\times768}$. Define the pre-normalized attention residual
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R(X,Y)=X+\operatorname{MHA}(\operatorname{LN}_q(X),\operatorname{LN}_{kv}(Y)),
\end{equation}
where $X$ supplies queries and $Y$ supplies keys and values, and let $\mathcal F(X)=X+\operatorname{FFN}(\operatorname{LN}(X))$. Four layers, indexed by $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,4\}$, update
\begin{align}
B_\ell&=\mathcal R_{B,\ell}(B_{\ell-1},M),\\
\widetilde Q_\ell&=\mathcal F_{1,\ell}\bigl(\mathcal R_{Q,\ell}(Q_{\ell-1},B_\ell)\bigr),\\
Q_\ell&=\mathcal F_{2,\ell}\bigl(\mathcal R_{S,\ell}(\widetilde Q_\ell,\widetilde Q_\ell)\bigr).
\end{align}
Each layer updates the bottleneck from visual memory, then applies query-to-bottleneck cross-attention and query self-attention. States $B_\ell$ and $Q_\ell$ carry across layers; only $B_0$ is a shared learned initialization. Attention uses eight heads. Query FFNs expand the width fourfold with GELU and dropout; the bottleneck update has no FFN.

Writing $Q_4=[s_{4,1},\ldots,s_{4,196},p_4]$, the pose head concatenates the final pose token with the mean of the 196 spatial tokens
\begin{equation}
z_{\rm pose}=[p_4;\operatorname{mean}_{j}s_{4,j}]\in\mathbb R^{1536}.
\end{equation}
A layer-normalized three-layer MLP maps this vector to two rotation components and two translations. The rotation components are normalized to form $(\cos\hat\theta,\sin\hat\theta)$.

\subsection{Contact Geometry Estimation}
\label{sec:decoupled}
To preserve local contact structure, the geometry path uses intermediate tactile features directly. Four feature maps from encoder blocks 3, 6, 9, and 12 bypass the pose-query trunk. After adding position embeddings, each map $T_i\in\mathbb R^{196\times768}$ receives context from the final visual bottleneck $B_4$:
\begin{equation}
T'_i=T_i+\alpha_i\,\operatorname{CA}_i(T_i,B_4),\qquad i=1,\ldots,4.
\label{eq:tap_inject}
\end{equation}
Each map has a separate pre-normalized cross-attention module $\operatorname{CA}_i$, with $T_i$ as queries and $B_4$ as key-values. The learned scalar gates $\alpha_i$ are initialized as zero, so visual conditioning is learned as a residual update to the tactile features. Both pose and geometry losses supervise the shared visual summary $B_4$.
The geometry decoder uses the conditioned spatial features directly, without depending on pose predictions. 

A DPT-style decoder~\cite{ranftl2021dpt} reassembles the four conditioned maps into 256-channel maps at $56^2$, $28^2$, $14^2$, and $7^2$ resolutions. Coarse-to-fine fusion produces a shared $112\times112$ feature map. Separate convolutional heads predict depth and surface normals at $224\times224$; predicted normal vectors are not explicitly normalized.

\subsection{Input Availability and Joint Training}
\label{sec:three_configs}
Modality dropout trains the same model with paired or individual inputs.
The Both mode follows the architecture above. In \emph{Vis}, learned mask queries replace tactile queries, and MAE maps from blocks 3, 6, 9, and 12 (projected from 1024 to 768 channels) supply the geometry features. In \emph{Tac}, both fusion cross-attention updates and the geometry path's visual conditioning are skipped.

All three modes receive pose and geometry supervision.
For inputs that include vision, geometry-path visual conditioning is independently enabled with probability $p_{\rm inject}$ during training and always enabled at inference.

Depth and normal losses are full-image MSEs. Rotation uses $1-\cos(\hat\theta-\theta)$, and translation uses coordinate-averaged $L_1$ error in normalized units. For $k\in\{D,\mathbf N,\mathrm{rot},\mathrm{trans}\}$, the uncertainty-weighted training objective~\cite{kendall2018multi} is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=\sum_k\left(e^{-s_k}\mathcal L_k+\tfrac12s_k\right),
\end{equation}
where each learned scalar $s_k$ weights one task.

\subsection{Sensor Observations and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
\noindent\textbf{Sensor observations and endurance}
Figure~\ref{fig:real_obj} illustrates common-view visual and tactile observations of four everyday objects. In the reported cyclic-loading test, a spherical indenter applied more than 45{,}000 normal indentations at 20\,N without observed cracking, delamination, or loss of the imaging-state change. This result characterizes the tested normal-loading protocol; sliding wear, contamination, and \mbox{optical drift were not quantified.}\unskip

\noindent\textbf{Data and geometric references}
A KUKA LBR Med R820 arm collects approximately 400 contacts for each of 12 objects, recording both image states and end-effector pose at every contact. A reference-object calibration relates object pose to the sensor frame. Pose and object geometry generate the depth reference maps; real normal maps are obtained by finite differences. These are computed geometric targets, not independent measurements of the deformed membrane. For the 12 corresponding geometries, Blender renders visual images, depth maps, and normal maps. Simulated tactile images are synthesized from the rendered depth maps, with random $\pm15\%$ depth scaling, by a depth-to-tactile generator (U-Net with a PatchGAN discriminator) trained only on real training pairs.\unskip

Both image streams are center-cropped to 0.816 of their original dimensions and resized to $224\times224$. Simulated visual images receive an additional $1.15\times$ center zoom to align their field of view with real images. Simulated poses are restricted to the per-object real rotation and translation ranges with a margin factor of 1.5. Simulation augmentation applies matched in-plane rotations within $\pm15^\circ$, together with channel, illumination, and noise perturbations. Real images are not augmented.

\noindent\textbf{Validation and checkpoint selection}
Every tenth sample within each real session is held out for validation and checkpoint selection. Objects and acquisition sessions are shared between training and validation, and object identity is supplied to the pose queries. All reported scores therefore measure within-session generalization on known objects.

Geometry and pose use separate checkpoints selected by depth MSE and mean angular error, respectively. Fusion models use both for selection; dedicated models use their native mode. Each selected fusion checkpoint is evaluated in all three modes without reselection, ensuring a common checkpoint for each within-task modality comparison.

\noindent\textbf{Geometry and pose metrics}
Depth and normal MSEs average over all pixels, including non-contact regions. Normal MSE also averages over its three components. Depth MSE is in $\mathrm{mm}^2$. Rotation $L_1$ sums the absolute cosine and sine errors per sample; angular error averages the inverse cosine of the clipped predicted--reference dot product, in degrees. Translation $L_1$ averages both coordinate errors, normalized by half the object's target size.

\noindent\textbf{Optimization}
The main fusion runs use real--simulation co-training at a real:sim ratio of 1:1.27. Optimization uses AdamW for 150 epochs with batch size 32, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.05, cosine decay, and 1{,}000 warmup steps. We use mixed precision and gradient clipping at 1.0. The input probabilities are $(p_{\rm Vis},p_{\rm Tac},p_{\rm Both})=(0.05,0.25,0.70)$, with $p_{\rm inject}=0.75$.

\noindent\textbf{Compared estimators}
All scores in Table~\ref{tab:main} are local evaluations. TVL~\cite{fu2024tvl} and Sparsh-X~\cite{higuera2025sparshx} replace the tactile encoder while retaining MAE and our downstream architecture. The Sparsh-X image encoder is locally pretrained on the co-training images and then frozen. VITaL~\cite{george2025vital} and MViTac~\cite{dave2024mvitac} use adapted ResNet-18 implementations; MViTac predicts pose only. Dedicated single-modality models attach geometry and pose heads to one frozen encoder. Symmetric MBT~\cite{nagrani2021mbt} replaces the fusion trunk while retaining the same encoders and heads.



\subsection{Complementary Contributions of Vision and Touch}
\label{sec:baselines}
In Table~\ref{tab:main}, pairing vision and touch reduces \method{}'s depth MSE to $0.0066\,\mathrm{mm}^2$, corresponding to reductions of 93.7\% relative to Vis and 61.2\% relative to Tac. Angular error decreases by 72.3\%, from $2.350^\circ$ with Tac to $0.652^\circ$ with Both, and is also lower than the $0.694^\circ$ obtained with Vis. Vision therefore provides substantially stronger pose information than touch alone, while both modalities contribute to contact-depth estimation.

Among the paired-input models in Table~\ref{tab:main}, \method{} achieves the lowest error across all five metrics. Compared with Sparsh-X+MAE, it reduces depth MSE by 38.3\% and normal MSE by 26.7\%, while lowering translation $L_1$ from 0.0055 to 0.0053. The largest improvements occur in geometry, whereas angular errors remain similar at $0.652^\circ$ for \method{} and $0.655^\circ$ for Sparsh-X+MAE.

\section{DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK}
\label{sec:conclusion_future_work}

\subsection{Discussion}

We presented \sensor{} and \method{} as a common-view visual--tactile sensing and estimation framework for object pose and contact geometry.

Our results suggest that the value of multimodal sensing lies not only in the distinct strengths of individual modalities, but also in their ability to resolve each other's ambiguities. Proximity vision provides a global prior for object pose and contact interpretation, whereas tactile sensing supplies a direct local measurement of contact geometry. The further improvement obtained with paired observations indicates cross-modal synergy rather than a simple division of sensing roles, motivating task-specific asymmetric fusion and state-dependent mode selection for dexterous manipulation.

Our real--simulation co-training study further reveals a trade-off between synthetic-data coverage and domain fidelity. Adding simulated observations expands the range of object poses and contact geometries seen during training and improves real-world validation performance over real-only training. However, residual sim--real discrepancies---including differences in illumination, blur, and background appearance in proximity mode that are difficult to eliminate---can introduce simulation-specific cues. As simulated samples account for a larger fraction of training, these discrepancies may increasingly bias the learned representation away from the real-data distribution, resulting in negative transfer and an intermediate optimal mixing ratio rather than monotonic gains from additional simulation. In our setting, the selected real-to-simulation ratio of 1:1.27 provides the best overall trade-off for pose estimation while retaining competitive geometry accuracy. This optimum is specific to the current simulator, dataset, and training configuration; more broadly, the result suggests that effective sim--real co-training must balance synthetic coverage against domain fidelity.



\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\noindent\textbf{Miniaturization and thermal management}
The current prototype retains the GelSlim~4.0 body; future work will co-design the camera--lens module, illumination, electronics, and contact stack for a smaller, power-efficient fingertip while preserving field of view and contact sensitivity. We will quantify package size, energy per paired observation, and contact-surface temperature under matched acquisition conditions.\unskip

\noindent\textbf{Perception evaluation}
Future work will evaluate cross-session generalization and validate local contact geometry using contact-region metrics and direct membrane-shape measurements.\unskip
